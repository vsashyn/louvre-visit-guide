# The Law Code of Hammurabi

Babylonian, c. 1755–1750 BC. Richelieu wing, Level 0, Room 227.

## In one line

A two-and-a-quarter-metre finger of black basalt covered in around 4,130 lines of cuneiform, and it is where "an eye for an eye" comes from.

## What you are looking at

A tapering stele of black basalt, polished smooth. At the top, a relief: Hammurabi, King of Babylon, stands before Shamash, the sun god and god of justice, who sits on a throne with flames rising from his shoulders. Shamash holds out a rod and a ring, the symbols of kingship and measurement. Hammurabi raises one hand to his mouth in respect.

Below the relief, the rest of the stone is text, written top to bottom in horizontal bands read right to left, in an already-archaic monumental cuneiform script.

The text is roughly one fifth prologue and epilogue, four fifths law. There are about 282 provisions.

### What the laws actually say

They are case law, phrased as "if a man does X, then Y". They cover theft, agriculture, damage to property, trade, debt, deposit, marriage, divorce, inheritance, adoption, assault, medical malpractice and building standards.

- Laws 196 and 200 are the lex talionis: if a man puts out the eye of a freeman, his eye shall be put out; if he knocks out the tooth of a freeman, his tooth shall be knocked out.
- Crucially, the penalty depends on class. There are three: awīlum, free gentlemen, muškēnum, commoners, and wardum and amtum, enslaved men and women. Putting out a commoner's eye costs money. Putting out a gentleman's eye costs an eye.
- The first two laws punish false accusation, which functions as a presumption of innocence.
- Interest is capped at 20 per cent. Prices and minimum seasonal wages are set.
- If a builder builds a house and it collapses and kills the owner, the builder is put to death.

## The story

Hammurabi had several copies carved and set up across his kingdom so the law was publicly visible. Around 1155 BC, six hundred years later, the Elamite king Shutruk-Nakhunte raided Babylonia and carried this one off to Susa, in what is now south-western Iran, as war loot.

The French archaeological mission under Jacques de Morgan excavated it at Susa between December 1901 and January 1902, in three pieces.

## Look for

- The erased panel. Seven columns near the bottom have been polished flat. Shutruk-Nakhunte almost certainly intended to carve his own inscription there and never did.
- The relief at the top. Shamash is handing over the authority to rule, not the laws themselves. Hammurabi is claiming to have received the right to legislate.
- The script. It is deliberately old-fashioned, the Babylonian equivalent of using blackletter to look venerable.

## Interesting facts

- It predates the laws of the Hebrew Bible by several centuries.
- It is not the oldest law code. The Code of Ur-Nammu is about three centuries earlier. Hammurabi's is the most complete.
- Copies of the text were still being made by scribes as a school exercise a thousand years after Hammurabi died.
- Hammurabi's image is carved on the wall of the United States House of Representatives chamber, among the lawgivers.

## Talking point

The relief takes up a fourteenth of the stone and the text takes the rest. Ask which one the king thought was doing the work.
