# The Gebel el-Arak Knife

Egyptian, c. 3450–3400 BC. Sully wing, Level 1, Room 633, in a case.

## In one line

Twenty-five centimetres of flint and ivory, older than the pyramids by nearly a thousand years, and it carries evidence that Egypt and Mesopotamia were in contact before either had a pharaoh.

## What you are looking at

A ripple-flaked flint blade, worked with a skill that modern knappers struggle to reproduce, fitted to a handle of elephant ivory carved on both faces in minute relief.

One face shows a bearded man in Mesopotamian dress, wearing a rolled cap and a long robe, standing between two rearing lions and mastering both. This is the Master of Animals, a motif from Uruk-period Mesopotamia, not from Egypt.

The other face shows a battle. Shaven-headed warriors fight long-haired opponents. Below them, two rows of boats: some with high curved prows of Mesopotamian type, some flat Egyptian reed craft. Dead men float in the water between them.

## The story

Georges Bénédite, an Egyptologist at the Louvre, bought it in February 1914 from the Cairo dealer Maurice Nahman. The blade and the handle arrived separately, because the dealer had not realised they belonged together.

The findspot, Gebel el-Arak in Upper Egypt, is recorded but unverified, which is normal for objects that came through the antiquities market.

## Look for

- The ripple flaking on the blade. Parallel flake scars across the whole surface, done by pressure flaking.
- The Master of Animals figure. Note the cap and the beard. Neither is Egyptian.
- The boat battle. Count the hulls and compare the prow shapes.
- The scale. You will need to get close and the carving is the size of a fingernail in places.

## Interesting facts

- Use-wear analysis shows the knife was never used. It is ceremonial.
- Flinders Petrie compared it to the Bayeux Tapestry and called it a national monument of conquest.
- The ivory is elephant, not hippopotamus as was long believed.
- The Mesopotamian iconography is one of the clearest pieces of evidence for contact between the Nile and Mesopotamia in the fourth millennium BC, before Egyptian unification.

## Talking point

This predates the Great Pyramid by about 900 years. The gap between this knife and Tutankhamun is longer than the gap between Tutankhamun and you.
