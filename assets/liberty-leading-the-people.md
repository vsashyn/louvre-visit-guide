# Liberty Leading the People

Eugène Delacroix, 1830. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 700 (Salle Mollien).

## In one line

Not the French Revolution. A three-day street fight in July 1830, painted three months after it happened.

## What you are looking at

A woman in a Phrygian cap strides over a barricade of paving stones and corpses, holding the tricolour in her right hand and a bayonetted musket in her left. Her dress has fallen off one shoulder. Around her: a boy with two pistols, a man in a top hat with a hunting gun, a worker with a sabre, a wounded man reaching up. Behind, the towers of Notre-Dame through gunsmoke.

She is an allegory, Liberty, and she is also a physically present woman with dirt on her feet and hair under her arms. That combination is what made the painting radical.

## The story

In July 1830 Charles X suspended press freedom and dissolved the chamber. Paris rose for three days, the Trois Glorieuses, and the king fell. Delacroix wrote to his brother: "I've embarked on a modern subject, a barricade."

The new government of Louis-Philippe bought it in 1831 for 3,000 francs and hung it in the throne room. After the June Rebellion of 1832 it was taken down and hidden in an attic for being too inflammatory. The Third Republic finally put it in the Louvre in 1874.

## Look for

- The boy with the pistols, later widely believed to be the model for Gavroche in Victor Hugo's Les Misérables.
- The man in the top hat. The old story that this is Delacroix himself is rejected by modern scholars. One candidate is the theatre director Étienne Arago.
- The dead soldier bottom left, stripped of his trousers. Delacroix painted the looting too.
- The towers of Notre-Dame in the haze at the right, with a tiny tricolour flying from one of them.

## Interesting facts

- The 2024 restoration found that Liberty's dress was originally light grey with gold, not the solid yellow it had become under old varnish, and revealed a boot in the lower left that had been invisible.
- A woman wrote "AE911" on the canvas at Louvre-Lens on 7 February 2013. Restorers removed it in under two hours with no damage to the original paint.
- It was on the French 100-franc note from 1978 to 1995, and on the cover of Coldplay's Viva la Vida in 2008.
- It was recreated as a tableau at the 2024 Paris Olympics opening ceremony.
- Bartholdi's Statue of Liberty descends from this figure.

## Talking point

The painting is a republican icon that was bought by a king, then hidden by that king because it worked too well.
