# Pastoral Concert

Titian, c. 1509–1510. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 711 (Salle des États), on the Venetian wall.

## In one line

The painting Manet was looking at when he decided to put a naked woman in a picnic and detonate French art.

## What you are looking at

Two clothed young men sit in a meadow, one playing a lute. Two nude women are with them, one pouring water from a glass jug into a well, one holding a recorder. In the distance a shepherd moves his flock, and there is a farmhouse and a sky heavy with weather.

Nobody in the picture looks at anybody else. The women may not be physically present at all. They are usually read as muses, visible only to the players.

## The story

Attributed to Giorgione until the twentieth century, now generally given to the young Titian, with a minority arguing for Domenico Mancini. The uncertainty is itself informative. The two painters were working side by side in Venice in exactly these years.

The provenance reads like a map of European power: the Gonzaga in Mantua, sold to Charles I of England around 1627, auctioned by the Commonwealth after his execution in 1649, bought by the banker Eberhard Jabach, sold to Louis XIV in 1671, into the Louvre in 1792.

## Look for

- The glass jug and the thread of water. Titian painting transparency for its own sake.
- How the two women are lit differently from the two men, as if they belong to a different order of reality.
- The storm light in the upper left.

## Interesting facts

- Édouard Manet saw this in the Louvre and produced Le Déjeuner sur l'herbe in 1863, which caused a scandal at the Salon des Refusés. The composition, the clothed men and nude woman, the picnic, all come from here.
- It was painted during the War of the League of Cambrai, when Venice was under real threat. The idyll may be a deliberate refuge.
- The painting is usually credited with launching the pastoral genre in European art.
- Delacroix, Degas and Fantin-Latour all copied it.

## Talking point

Walk to Manet at the Musée d'Orsay later in the trip and the line between the two paintings is unmissable.
