# The Borghese Gladiator

Agasias of Ephesus, c. 100 BC. Sully wing, Level 0, in the Greek galleries near the Venus de Milo.

## In one line

Not a gladiator. A warrior fighting a man on horseback, and for two centuries he was the reference book European sculptors used for the human body.

## What you are looking at

A nude man lunging forward along a violent diagonal, left arm raised high with a shield strap still visible, right arm thrown back. Every muscle in the body is engaged and legible. He is looking up and to his left, which tells you his opponent is mounted.

The figure is carved to be read from one main viewpoint, along the line of the lunge.

## The story

Found before 1611 in the territory of Anzio, south of Rome, among the ruins of a seaside villa of Nero. Cardinal Scipione Borghese acquired it and it became one of the most admired antiquities in Europe.

Camillo Borghese sold the Borghese antiquities to his brother-in-law Napoleon in 1807, which is how a huge part of the Louvre's classical sculpture arrived in one transaction.

The name is wrong. A seventeenth-century restoration and a misreading turned a warrior into a gladiator, and the label stuck.

## Look for

- The signature on the tree-trunk support: AGASIAS, SON OF DOSITHEOS, EPHESIAN, MADE IT. Signed ancient sculpture is rare.
- The raised left arm, which once held a shield against a cavalry attack.
- The back. The musculature of the shoulder blades and lower back is why sculptors made casts of this figure for three hundred years.

## Interesting facts

- Bronze casts were installed at Windsor Castle, Houghton Hall, Petworth, Castle Howard and Knole. Thomas Jefferson wanted one at Monticello.
- In the eighteenth century the figure supplied a canon of proportions taught in art academies.
- Rubens used the pose in the Marie de' Medici cycle, which hangs in the Richelieu wing of this museum, after seeing the original in Rome.
- Friedrich Thiersch suggested the opponent was the Amazon Penthesilea and the warrior Achilles.
- Thomas Cole based the headless statue in his 1836 painting Destruction on it.

## Talking point

Stand where the horseman would be. The whole sculpture is built around an opponent who was never carved.
