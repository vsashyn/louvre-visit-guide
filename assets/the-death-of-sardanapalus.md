# The Death of Sardanapalus

Eugène Delacroix, 1827. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 700 (Salle Mollien).

## In one line

A king watching his own possessions, including his women and horses, being destroyed on his orders, painted so violently that it nearly ended Delacroix's career.

## What you are looking at

The Assyrian king Sardanapalus, besieged and about to lose, reclines on an enormous red bed and watches his eunuchs and guards kill his concubines and slaughter his horse before the palace is set alight. He is the only still thing in the picture.

The composition has no stable centre. It tips down and to the left along the diagonal of the bed and spills out of the frame at the bottom. The dominant colour is a red so saturated it reads as heat.

## The story

Delacroix took the subject from Byron's 1821 verse drama Sardanapalus, which drew on the Greek historian Diodorus Siculus. Byron's king is a reluctant hedonist. Delacroix's is something colder.

At the 1827 Salon it was savaged. One critic called it "the fanaticism of ugliness". The state told Delacroix that if he wanted further official commissions he would need to change direction. He was 29.

## Look for

- The king's face. Utterly detached, propped on one hand.
- The horse in the lower left, being pulled down by a groom. Delacroix was a lifelong painter of horses.
- The lack of a stable floor. You cannot work out where the bed sits in the room.
- The jewels, gold plate and elephant-head bedposts, painted with as much care as the bodies.

## Interesting facts

- Linda Nochlin argued the painting scandalised viewers because it collapses the safe distance between the exotic Other and the Western viewer.
- Hector Berlioz wrote a cantata on Sardanapalus in 1830. Liszt started an opera and finished only Act 1, which was not performed until 2018.
- Delacroix painted a smaller second version in 1844, now in the Philadelphia Museum of Art.
- The extreme foreshortening pushes the violence toward you rather than framing it at a distance.

## Talking point

Stand close enough that the canvas fills your field of view. That is roughly the experience the 1827 critics were objecting to.
