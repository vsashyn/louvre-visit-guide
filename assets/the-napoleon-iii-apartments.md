# The Napoleon III Apartments

Hector Lefuel, 1854–1861. Richelieu wing, Level 1, Room 544 onward.

## In one line

Eleven rooms of crimson velvet, gilt and crystal, and Napoleon III never slept a night in them.

## What you are looking at

A complete Second Empire state apartment: a grand salon, a theatre salon, a dining room seating a large party, smaller drawing rooms, all of it intact. Around 900 square metres. Gold leaf everywhere, crimson silk on the walls, parquet, mirrors, chandeliers.

These were not private rooms. They were built for the Minister of State to entertain on behalf of the Emperor. Napoleon III himself lived across the road at the Tuileries Palace, which was burned down by the Commune in 1871 and demolished.

### The Grand Salon

The ceiling painting by Charles-Raphaël Maréchal, made around 1860, shows the Reuniting of the Louvre and the Tuileries by Napoleon III, which is the building work you are standing inside.

The chandelier is 4.7 by 3.27 metres and carries 180 lights.

The furniture is worth attention. Small gilt chairs designed to be picked up and moved. A round bourne sofa in the middle of the room for lounging. An indiscret, a three-seat armchair arranged so that three people can talk without any of them facing the same way.

## The story

Built between 1854 and 1861 as part of Napoleon III's vast expansion of the Louvre, the biggest single building campaign the palace ever had.

After the Second Empire fell in 1870, the Ministry of Finance moved in and stayed for 119 years. It only left in 1989, when Mitterrand's Grand Louvre project turned the whole building over to the museum.

The apartments opened to the public in 1993. They closed in September 2023 for a nine-month restoration and reopened in June 2024.

## Look for

- The theatre salon and the musicians' gallery over the passage between rooms.
- The dining room table, laid.
- The upholstery and the passementerie, the braid and tassels, which were remade from surviving originals.
- The ceiling in the Grand Salon, which depicts the building of the wing you are in.

## Interesting facts

- The Second Empire lasted eighteen years. This is one of the most complete surviving interiors from it anywhere.
- Hector Lefuel also designed the Richelieu and Denon pavilions on the Cour Napoléon, the facades you walk past on the way in.
- Most visitors never find these rooms. They are on the same floor as the decorative arts, which most people skip.

## Practical

Allow twenty-five minutes. Wear something you do not mind photographing yourself in, because the mirrors are unavoidable.

## Talking point

The Tuileries Palace, where the Emperor actually lived, is gone. The gap at the west end of the Louvre where it stood is now the Jardin du Carrousel.
