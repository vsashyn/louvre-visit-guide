# The Daru Staircase

Hector Lefuel, 1855, completed by Albert Ferran in 1934. Denon wing, between Levels 0 and 1, Room 703.

## In one line

The best piece of theatre in the building, and it exists to make one statue land.

## What you are looking at

A monumental stone staircase rising from the Denon ground floor. It splits, turns, and delivers you onto a landing where the Winged Victory of Samothrace stands on her ship's prow, backlit, at the top of the flight.

The staircase was designed by Lefuel in 1855 as part of Napoleon III's expansion. The Victory was installed at the top in 1884, and the two have been a single composition ever since. Albert Ferran completed the upper section in 1934.

## The story

The Victory was originally placed in a rock niche high above the sanctuary at Samothrace, designed to be seen from below and from her left as you climbed toward her. The Daru staircase reproduces that condition almost exactly, by accident or by very good instinct.

You come up the stairs. She appears above you, leaning into the wind, at the top of a flight of steps. That is what a Greek visitor to Samothrace got in the second century BC.

## Look for

- The approach. Do not take the escalators. Come up the stairs from the Denon ground floor.
- The turn where she first becomes visible.
- Her left side. Stand below and to her left, which is the angle the sculptor built for.
- The volume of the space above her, which is what makes the wings read.

## Interesting facts

- Lefuel designed the staircase in 1855, and the Victory only arrived at the top in 1884. They were not conceived together.
- Albert Ferran finished the upper landing in 1934, seventy-nine years after the lower flight was built.
- The staircase is named after Pierre Daru, Napoleon's intendant general, as is the gallery at the top of it.
- The right hand of the Victory is displayed in a case on the landing, reunited from a palm found on Samothrace in 1950 and fingers held in Vienna since the 1870s.

## Practical

This is one of the most photographed spots in Paris and it is crowded most of the day. Early morning or the last hour of the evening opening is when you get it clear.

## Talking point

Two thousand years apart, a Greek sanctuary and a Second Empire staircase produced the same effect. Ask whether that is design or luck.
