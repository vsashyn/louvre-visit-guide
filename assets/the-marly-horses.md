# The Marly Horses

Guillaume Coustou the Elder, 1743–1745. Richelieu wing, Level -1, Room 105, the Cour Marly.

## In one line

Two rearing horses and two grooms who are losing, carved in Carrara marble, standing under a glass roof in a courtyard built for them.

## What you are looking at

Two groups, each with a horse up on its hind legs and a near-naked groom straining to hold it. The horses are winning. Manes and tails fly, nostrils flare, hooves come off the base, and the grooms are twisted into positions of pure effort.

Coustou carved them at the end of his career, in his mid-sixties. They were his last major work.

## The story

They were made for the horse pond at the entrance to the grounds of Louis XV's château at Marly, replacing an earlier pair by Antoine Coysevox, which had themselves been moved to the Tuileries.

In 1794 the Revolutionary government moved them to the entrance of the Champs-Élysées at the Place de la Concorde, where they stood for 190 years, framing one of the most photographed views in Paris.

Bastille Day military parades, pollution and weather took a heavy toll. In 1984 the originals came indoors to the Louvre, and Michel Bourbon's marble copies went up at the Concorde. The courtyard they now occupy was glassed over in the 1990s and named the Cour Marly in their honour.

## Look for

- The grooms' hands and feet, where the strain is concentrated.
- The undercutting. Manes, tails and reins are carved free of the block, which on this scale in marble is a serious risk.
- The bases. They are worked into rock, waves and shells, since the groups stood at a water trough.
- The whole courtyard. It is three tiered levels of French sculpture from Louis XIV's Paris squares below, Versailles garden sculpture in the middle, and the Marly groups at the top.

## Interesting facts

- They were on French television test cards from 1953 to 1983, so a generation of French viewers saw them every day.
- Louis-Denis Caillouette restored them in 1840.
- The Cour Marly and its neighbour the Cour Puget were open courtyards until the Grand Louvre works of the 1990s put glass roofs over them. They are among the most pleasant spaces in the museum and are almost always quiet.
- Coysevox's earlier Marly horses, with Fame and Mercury riding winged steeds, are also here.

## Practical

This courtyard has natural light, benches and space. If you need a break from the crowds in Denon, come here.

## Talking point

The copies at the Place de la Concorde have been there since 1984. Most Parisians have never seen the originals.
