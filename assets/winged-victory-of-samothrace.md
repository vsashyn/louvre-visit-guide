# The Winged Victory of Samothrace

Greek, c. 200–190 BC. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 703, at the head of the Daru staircase.

## In one line

She has no head and no arms, and she is still the best-staged object in the building.

## What you are looking at

Nike, the winged messenger of victory, landing on the prow of a warship. Her right leg takes the impact, her left trails, her wings are still braced against the air. The wind has flattened her chiton against her body and torn the cloak away behind her.

The drapery is the point. Greek sculptors called the effect wet drapery, and here it does two contradictory things at once: it clings to show the body, and it whips loose to show the speed.

## The story

Found on Samothrace on 13 April 1863 by the French diplomat Charles Champoiseau, in the sanctuary of the Great Gods, where sailors prayed for protection at sea. She originally stood high above the sanctuary in a rock niche, angled so she was seen in three-quarter view from her left.

Champoiseau shipped the fragments to Paris. The statue was reassembled without wings and shown to a public that took little interest. About ten years later archaeologists realised that grey marble blocks found nearby were not rubble but a ship's prow. She was rebuilt with the prow and with a reconstructed right wing, and that is when she became famous.

She has stood at the top of the Daru staircase since 1884.

## Look for

- The prow. It is carved with real naval detail, and it is Lartos marble from Rhodes, which is why Rhodes is thought to have dedicated the monument.
- The right wing is a plaster reconstruction, mirrored from the surviving left wing. Look for the join.
- Her right hand, displayed separately in a case nearby. The palm was found on Samothrace in July 1950 and matched to fingers that had been in Vienna since the 1870s.
- The angle. Stand to her left and slightly below, which is the view she was designed for.

## Interesting facts

- The 2013–2014 conservation found traces of blue pigment on the wings and on a band at the bottom of the mantle. She was painted.
- The hand is open with the fingers extended, which rules out the old reconstruction that had her holding a trumpet. She was probably waving.
- She has been at the top of that staircase for over 140 years, which is longer than most nations' constitutions.
- The head has never been found.

## Practical

Go up the Daru staircase rather than the escalators. The whole installation is built for the approach from below.

## Talking point

Nothing about her is symmetrical. Find the axis she is actually built on.
