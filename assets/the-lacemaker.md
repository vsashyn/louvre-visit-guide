# The Lacemaker

Johannes Vermeer, c. 1669–1671. Richelieu wing, Level 2, Room 837.

## In one line

Vermeer's smallest painting, about the size of a sheet of A4, and Renoir called it the most beautiful picture in the world.

## What you are looking at

A young woman bent forward over a lace pillow, holding a pair of bobbins in her right hand and a pin in her left. She wears a yellow bodice. Her hair is pinned back in tight curls. Behind her, nothing at all: a blank cream wall.

In front of her, a blue sewing cushion spills red and white threads over the edge of the table, and those threads are painted completely out of focus, as loose blobs of colour with no drawn edge.

### The blur

That deliberate blur, against the needle-sharp lace under her fingers, is the painting's whole argument. Vermeer gives you depth of field, the optical effect you get from a lens, not from an eye.

It is one of the strongest pieces of evidence that Vermeer used a camera obscura. Not to trace, but to see. A lens shows you that the world has focal planes, and Vermeer painted that.

## The story

Vermeer painted around 34 surviving works. The Louvre has two, this and The Astronomer, hanging in the same room.

The canvas comes from the same bolt of cloth as A Young Woman Seated at the Virginals, which suggests the two paintings were once the same size, and that this one may have been trimmed.

The Louvre bought it in 1870.

## Look for

- The threads in the foreground, and how little actual painting is there.
- The lace itself. The finest brushwork in the picture is on her hands.
- The light on the wall, which has almost no incident in it and is still not flat.
- The book at her elbow, probably a Bible, which has been read as making her work a virtuous domestic activity.

## Interesting facts

- Renoir said it and Watteau's Embarkation for Cythera were the two most beautiful paintings in the world.
- Salvador Dalí was obsessed with it. He produced a series of interpretations in 1955, including The Paranoiac-Critical Study of Vermeer's Lacemaker, and filmed himself copying it at the Louvre with a rhinoceros.
- Lawrence Gowing wrote that the picture achieves "a complete and single definition" and stands alone in Vermeer's work.
- Bobbin lace was a real and valuable Dutch industry. This is a working woman, not an allegory.

## Talking point

It is smaller than a laptop screen and there is usually a bench in front of it. Sit down.
