# Pilgrimage to the Island of Cythera

Jean-Antoine Watteau, 1717. Sully wing, Level 2, French eighteenth-century painting.

## In one line

The painting that forced the French Academy to invent a new category because it did not fit any of the existing ones.

## What you are looking at

A wooded slope above a bay in golden late light. Couples move down the hill toward a gilded boat with a rose-coloured sail. Cupids swarm above the mast. At the right, beside a garlanded herm of Venus, a couple are still seated; next to them a man is helping a woman to her feet; further on, she looks back over her shoulder at the place they are leaving.

Cythera is the island where Venus rose from the sea, the mythical destination of lovers.

### Arriving or leaving?

Despite the title, the pilgrims look like they are departing. They have already paired up. The couple at the right are only now getting to their feet, and the woman glances back. Read left to right, the picture is an embarkation. Read right to left, it is a single couple shown in three successive moments, from seduction to departure, like frames of a film.

Watteau refused to settle it, and the argument has run for three hundred years.

## The story

Watteau was admitted to the Académie Royale in 1712 on the condition that he submit a reception piece. He took five years and had to be formally reminded. He delivered this in August 1717.

The Academy had a fixed hierarchy of genres: history painting at the top, then portraits, then genre, landscape, still life. This picture was not a history painting and not a landscape. The Academy created a new category for it, fête galante, elegant outdoor entertainment, and admitted him under it.

Watteau was 33 and had four years to live. He died of tuberculosis in 1721.

## Look for

- The three-stage couple on the right, and whether you read them as one couple or three.
- The herm of Venus, garlanded with roses, with a quiver hanging on it.
- The woman looking back. She is the emotional centre of the picture.
- The paint. Watteau applied it thinly and quickly, and the surface has a nervous, sketched quality unlike anything in French painting before.
- The light, which is the light of a day ending.

## Interesting facts

- Watteau painted a second, richer version around 1718–1719, now at Charlottenburg Palace in Berlin. An earlier and simpler treatment of the subject is in Frankfurt.
- During the Revolution the painting was damaged by students at the Academy who used it for target practice with bread pellets.
- It fell out of fashion completely and was only rehabilitated in the 1830s when the Goncourt brothers and others revived interest in the Rococo.
- Debussy's piano piece L'isle joyeuse of 1904 may have been inspired by it.
- Renoir named it and Vermeer's Lacemaker as the two most beautiful paintings in the world.

## Talking point

Decide whether they are going or coming back. Then look at the woman glancing over her shoulder and decide again.
