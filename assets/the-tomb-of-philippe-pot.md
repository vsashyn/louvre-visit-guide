# The Tomb of Philippe Pot

Burgundian, c. 1477–1483. Richelieu wing, Level 0, Room 210.

## In one line

Eight life-size hooded mourners carry a dead knight on their shoulders, and they have been carrying him for five hundred and forty years.

## What you are looking at

A recumbent knight in full armour, hands joined in prayer, a lion at his feet. The slab he lies on is not resting on a base. It is being carried, at shoulder height, by eight standing figures in long black hooded robes, their faces completely hidden.

Each mourner holds a painted heraldic shield in front of him, showing the arms of one of Philippe Pot's ancestral families. Each one moves slightly differently.

The whole weight of the slab rests on a narrow point on each mourner's shoulder. Structurally it should not work.

## The story

Philippe Pot, 1428 to 1493, was born at the Château de la Rochepot in Burgundy. He served the last two Valois Dukes of Burgundy, Philip the Good and Charles the Bold, and was made a knight of the Golden Fleece.

When Charles the Bold was killed at the Battle of Nancy in 1477, Burgundy collapsed. Pot switched to Louis XI of France, who made him grand seneschal of Burgundy. He was a survivor of a regime change and the tomb was commissioned in the years right after it.

### The mourners

Hooded pleurants were a Burgundian speciality. The tombs of the Dukes of Burgundy at Dijon have dozens of small alabaster mourners in an arcade under the effigy.

What is unprecedented here is scale and function. These are 134 to 144 cm tall, close to life size, and they are not decorative. They are structural. The mourners hold the tomb up.

## Look for

- The gaits. Each figure carries the weight slightly differently. Some lean, some are square.
- The heraldry on the eight shields, all his ancestral families.
- The faces. You cannot see any of them. The hoods are complete.
- The date of death on the inscription.

## Interesting facts

- Pot commissioned the tomb thirteen years before he died, and the death date was left blank while it was built.
- It was made for the chapel of Saint-Jean-Baptiste at Cîteaux Abbey near Dijon, the mother house of the Cistercian order.
- After the Revolution it passed through several hands before the Louvre acquired it in 1889.
- It was comprehensively restored between 2016 and 2018, stripping later gloss layers and unsuitable repairs.
- It influenced funerary sculpture across Europe and changed how mourners were used in tomb design.

## Talking point

Walk around it once at a slow pace. The figures are the right height that you will feel like part of the procession.
