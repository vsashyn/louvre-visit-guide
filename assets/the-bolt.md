# The Bolt

Jean-Honoré Fragonard, c. 1777–1778. Sully wing, Level 2, French eighteenth-century painting.

## In one line

More than half the canvas is a curtain, and the actual subject is a man's hand reaching for a door bolt.

## What you are looking at

A darkened bedroom. A young man, half-undressed, has one arm around a woman and is stretching the other up and to the right to shoot the bolt on the door. She pushes against his face with one hand and her body arcs away, or leans in, depending on how you read it.

On the left, occupying the whole other half of the painting, a bed with a vast mass of crumpled sheets and a red canopy whose folds spill out past the frame.

A single hard diagonal of light runs from the bottom left to the bolt in the top right corner. Everything in the picture points at it.

### The ambiguity

Is this seduction or assault? Her hand is on his face. Her body is turning away. Her feet are off the floor. But her arm is also around his shoulder, and eighteenth-century pictorial convention read the pose as resistance that is expected to be overcome, which is its own problem.

Fragonard leaves it unresolved, and modern viewers resolve it differently from the way the Marquis de Véri's guests would have.

## The story

Commissioned in 1773 by Louis-Gabriel Véri-Raionard, Marquis de Véri, a serious and demanding collector. Véri hung it as a pendant to Fragonard's Adoration of the Shepherds, which is an extraordinary pairing: the sacred and the profane, the same size, facing each other.

Fragonard originally planned it as one of three canvases with The Contract and The Armoire, telling a story in three chapters.

The Louvre acquired it in 1974.

## Look for

- The apple on the table at the left, lit by the same shaft of light. Eve, temptation, the fall, stated without subtlety.
- The overturned chair.
- The sheets. This is the most virtuosic passage of paint in the room and it is entirely about fabric.
- The upset flowers and the spilled objects, all signs of disorder.

## Interesting facts

- Fragonard had won the Prix de Rome and painted serious history subjects. The erotic pictures were a deliberate commercial pivot and they are what he is remembered for.
- Within fifteen years the Revolution had made this kind of painting politically toxic. Fragonard survived it, partly thanks to his former pupil Jacques-Louis David, and died poor and forgotten in 1806.
- Note where it hangs in relation to David's Oath of the Horatii, painted seven years later. The break is total.

## Talking point

Cover the right half of the painting. What is left is one of the best pieces of pure fabric painting in the museum.
