# The Virgin and Child with Saint Anne

Leonardo da Vinci, c. 1501–1519. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 710 (Grande Galerie).

## In one line

Leonardo worked on this for nearly twenty years and still never finished it, and it is arguably the most technically ambitious thing he ever attempted.

## What you are looking at

Three generations stacked into one shape. Saint Anne sits, her daughter Mary sits on her lap, and Mary leans forward to catch the infant Jesus, who is climbing onto a sacrificial lamb. The lamb is the Passion. Mary is trying to hold him back from it and cannot.

Behind them, a blue mountain landscape dissolves into haze. Leonardo's sfumato is at full strength here, and so is his interest in atmospheric perspective.

## The story

Louis XII of France probably commissioned it around 1499, after the birth of his daughter. It was never delivered. Leonardo carried it with him, reworking it, and it was still in his possession in France at his death in 1519.

## Look for

- The impossible seating. Mary is a grown woman on her mother's lap, and Leonardo makes it read as natural through sheer compositional skill. Squint and the three figures resolve into a single spiralling mass.
- The unfinished passages. Anne's feet and parts of the drapery are barely blocked in.
- Anne's smile, the same withheld expression as the Mona Lisa.
- The tree at the right, painted with a botanist's precision against a background of pure atmosphere.

## Interesting facts

- In 2008 a Louvre curator using infrared reflectography found faint drawings on the back of the panel: a horse's head about 18 by 10 cm, a skull, and a study of the Christ child. They may be the only known Leonardo drawings on the reverse of one of his paintings.
- Sigmund Freud wrote an entire essay on this painting, arguing the Virgin's blue drapery hides the shape of a vulture, which he tied to a childhood memory Leonardo recorded of a bird landing on his cradle. Freud called it "the only beautiful thing I have ever written."
- Freud was working from a mistranslation. Leonardo wrote about a kite, not a vulture. The whole argument rests on a bad German edition.
- The 2011 cleaning triggered a public fight in which two members of the Louvre's own advisory committee resigned, arguing the painting had been left brighter than Leonardo intended.
- A related full-size cartoon, the Burlington House Cartoon, is in the National Gallery in London. It is a different composition, with John the Baptist instead of the lamb.

## Talking point

Count the legs. Leonardo does not entirely resolve where Mary's lower body goes, and once you see it you cannot unsee it.
