# The Pietà of Villeneuve-lès-Avignon

Enguerrand Quarton, c. 1455–1460. Richelieu wing, Level 2, French fifteenth-century painting.

## In one line

Widely called the greatest French painting of the fifteenth century, and it took art historians until the 1930s to work out who painted it.

## What you are looking at

The dead Christ lies across his mother's knees, his body bent into a hard arc that nobody had drawn that way before. Mary Magdalene kneels at the right with an ointment jar. Saint John the Evangelist is at the left, gently lifting the crown of thorns from Christ's head.

At the far left, a kneeling donor in white clerical dress, painted with a completely different, sharply observed Netherlandish realism, looking past the group rather than at it.

The whole ground is beaten gold, with haloes and inscriptions incised and punched into it. Along the horizon, a city skyline of domes and towers.

### What makes it unusual

The Virgin does not clutch the body. Her hands are folded in prayer. She is not performing grief, she is enduring it.

The curve of Christ's body is a deliberate formal device, not an attempt at anatomy. It bends against the horizontals of the composition and produces the tension that holds the picture together.

## The story

Painted for the Carthusian monastery at Villeneuve-lès-Avignon, across the Rhône from Avignon. The attribution to Enguerrand Quarton, a painter from Picardy documented in Provence, was contested for decades after the painting was first exhibited in 1904, with scholars proposing Catalan, Portuguese and Italian origins. It is now generally accepted.

The Louvre acquired it in 1905.

## Look for

- The donor at the left, and how different in style he is from everything else in the panel.
- The skyline. It has been read as Jerusalem, and also as Constantinople with the dome of Hagia Sophia, which would make it a lament for the city that fell to the Ottomans in 1453.
- The tooling in the gold. Haloes, borders and inscriptions are all punched and incised.
- The inscription running around the frame area in gold, from Lamentations: "O all ye that pass by, behold and see if there be any sorrow like unto my sorrow."
- Mary Magdalene's tears, individually painted.

## Interesting facts

- Edward Lucie-Smith called it perhaps the greatest masterpiece produced in France in the fifteenth century.
- Avignon had been the seat of the papacy from 1309 to 1377, and remained a crossroads where Netherlandish, Italian and Spanish painting met. That mix is why the attribution was so hard.
- The French fifteenth-century rooms are among the emptiest galleries in the museum.

## Talking point

Everything in it is stylised except the donor, who looks like a photograph. Ask why the painter used two incompatible styles in one panel.
