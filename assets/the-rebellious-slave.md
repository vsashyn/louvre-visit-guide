# The Rebellious Slave

Michelangelo, 1513–1515. Denon wing, Level 0, Room 403 (Galerie Michel-Ange), beside the Dying Slave.

## In one line

The other half of the pair, and the one still fighting.

## What you are looking at

A powerfully built man with his hands bound behind his back, twisting his torso and raising one shoulder and one knee as he strains against the bonds. His head turns sharply and his face is contorted. Much of the surface is left rough, and the block he stands on is barely worked.

Where the Dying Slave sinks, this one pushes. The pair are a single argument in two figures.

## The story

Same commission as its companion: the second design for the tomb of Julius II, 1513. Same fate, dropped from the final monument. Same journey to France through Roberto Strozzi, Henri II, Montmorency at Écouen, Richelieu in Poitou, and Paris in 1749. Hidden during the Terror in 1793, then absorbed into the national collections.

Vasari thought the figures personified provinces conquered by Julius II. Michelangelo's other biographer Condivi thought they were the liberal arts, imprisoned at the Pope's death. Both were writing with access to Michelangelo and they disagree.

## Look for

- The rough chisel marks. Large areas are at the claw-chisel stage, several steps from finish, which lets you read how Michelangelo worked in from the surface of the block.
- The raised shoulder and knee. They push out toward you, so the figure occupies more space than the block allows.
- The bonds, again carved in low relief and easy to miss.
- The unfinished head of a second figure, barely begun, low on the block behind him.

## Interesting facts

- The non-finito, Michelangelo's habit of leaving work incomplete, was read by later generations as a philosophical statement about the soul trapped in matter. In practice it usually meant he had moved on to another job.
- The tomb of Julius II took forty years and ended as a comparatively modest wall monument in San Pietro in Vincoli in Rome, dominated by the Moses.
- Four more unfinished Prigioni are in the Accademia in Florence, on the way to the David.

## Talking point

These two are the only Michelangelo sculptures in France. Everything else is in Italy.
