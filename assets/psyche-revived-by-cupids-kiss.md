# Psyche Revived by Cupid's Kiss

Antonio Canova, 1787–1793. Denon wing, Level 0, Room 403 (Galerie Michel-Ange), near the window.

## In one line

Two bodies making an X in white marble, and the whole thing rests on a base that used to rotate.

## What you are looking at

Cupid has just landed. His wings are still up. He kneels and lifts Psyche's head and shoulders, and she reaches up to draw his face toward hers. Their arms form a circle around a gap that their mouths have not yet closed.

Canova has caught the instant before the kiss, not the kiss. Everything in the piece is arrested motion.

## The story

From Apuleius's The Golden Ass. Venus, jealous, sends Psyche to the underworld to fetch a box from Proserpina and forbids her to open it. Psyche opens it. Inside is not beauty but the sleep of the dead, and it takes her. Cupid finds her, wipes the sleep from her face and wakes her with an arrow's touch, or in Canova's version a kiss.

Colonel John Campbell commissioned it in Rome in 1787. Joachim Murat, Napoleon's brother-in-law and later King of Naples, acquired it in 1800 for his château at Villiers. It entered the Louvre in 1824, after Murat's execution.

Canova carved a second version, sold to Prince Yusupov in Rome in 1796, now in the Hermitage.

## Look for

- The jar on the ground beside Psyche's hip, the box of Proserpina's sleep that caused all this.
- Cupid's quiver and bow lying on the rock.
- The hands. Psyche's fingers are in his hair; his are under her breast and behind her head. Four hands, four different jobs.
- The polish. Canova finished marble with abrasives and then rubbed the surface until it caught light like skin. His workshop reportedly used a wash to warm the white.

## Interesting facts

- The base originally had a rotating mechanism, worked by a handle near Psyche's feet, so a viewer could turn the group and see it from every side.
- The critic Carl Ludwig Fernow complained that it had no single ideal viewpoint and forced the viewer to run around it. That was Canova's intention.
- Canova initially refused to work for Napoleon, arguing that art stood above politics. Political pressure eventually won.
- Another Canova version of the subject, with the pair standing, is at Louvre-Lens.

## Talking point

Walk a full circle. Find the angle where their faces are closest to touching, and the angle where the composition looks most like a single body.
