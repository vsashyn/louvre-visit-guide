# Mona Lisa

Leonardo da Vinci, c. 1503–1519. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 711 (Salle des États).

## In one line

A silk merchant's wife from Florence, painted by a man who never delivered the portrait and never stopped working on it.

## What you are looking at

Lisa Gherardini, wife of the Florentine silk merchant Francesco del Giocondo, sits on a balcony in front of a landscape that does not exist. The bridge, the winding road and the blue-green valley are invented. Leonardo painted her on a single plank of white poplar, and the paint is applied in layers so thin that in places they are measured in fractions of a micron.

The technique everyone names is sfumato, Italian for "smoked". Leonardo blends tone into tone with no visible line, especially at the corners of the mouth and eyes. That is why the expression seems to change. Your eye cannot fix the edges, so your brain keeps re-reading them.

## The story

A note written in the margin of a book by the Florentine clerk Agostino Vespucci in October 1503 records that Leonardo was then at work on a portrait of Lisa del Giocondo. That single line is the strongest evidence for who she is.

Leonardo never handed the painting over. He carried it to France in 1516 when Francis I invited him to Amboise, and it stayed in the French royal collection. It hung at Fontainebleau, then Versailles, then in Napoleon's bedroom, before entering the Louvre after the Revolution.

## Look for

- The horizon lines on the left and right sides do not match. The right side sits higher, which subtly tilts her face.
- No eyebrows and no eyelashes. High-resolution scans from 2007 showed she originally had them, lost to cleaning.
- The crack running down from the top of the panel, stabilised centuries ago with butterfly braces on the back.
- The gauzy veil over her hair, a sign of a married woman, and the transparent gauze across the bodice.
- Her hands. The right resting over the left is one of the most copied gestures in portraiture.

## Interesting facts

- She was stolen on 21 August 1911 by Vincenzo Peruggia, a Louvre handyman who walked out with her under his smock. He kept her in his Paris flat for two years, then tried to sell her to the Uffizi in Florence out of Italian patriotism. She came back on 4 January 1914. He served six months.
- The empty wall where she had hung drew bigger crowds than the painting had. The theft is a large part of why she is the most famous painting on earth.
- Pablo Picasso was questioned by police as a suspect in the 1911 theft.
- She sits in a sealed case with controlled temperature and humidity because the poplar panel has warped over five centuries.
- Attacks: a rock in 1956 that chipped pigment near the left elbow, acid the same year, a ceramic teacup in 2009, cake in 2022, soup in 2024. Since the glass went up, nothing has touched the paint.
- Insured at 100 million dollars in 1962, which is why she holds the Guinness record for highest insurance valuation for a painting. In practice France does not insure her, because she is considered priceless and the state self-insures.
- The Louvre plans to move her to a purpose-built room under the Cour Carrée with its own entrance and ticket, part of the Louvre Nouvelle Renaissance project due around 2031. Until then she stays in Room 711.

## Practical

Room 711 runs a one-way queue. Join it and you get roughly 30 seconds at the barrier, about four metres away. Go at opening or in the last 90 minutes for the shortest wait. The glass reflects, so stand slightly off-centre.

Do not spend your energy here. Turn around instead. See the next file.

## Talking point

She is smaller than everyone expects, roughly the size of a large laptop screen. Ask which of you guessed closer before you see her.
