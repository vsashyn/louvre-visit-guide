# The Dying Slave

Michelangelo, 1513–1515. Denon wing, Level 0, Room 403 (Galerie Michel-Ange).

## In one line

Not dying and not a slave, and Michelangelo never finished him.

## What you are looking at

A young man, nude, one arm raised behind his head, the other across his chest, his head fallen back and his eyes closed. A narrow band crosses his chest, carved in low relief. His weight sinks. He looks less like a man in pain than a man in the moment before sleep takes him.

Behind his left leg, half emerging from the uncut block, there is a monkey.

## The story

Michelangelo carved him for the second design of the tomb of Pope Julius II, commissioned by the Della Rovere heirs in May 1513. The project was cut down repeatedly over forty years and by the final 1542 version both this figure and its companion had been dropped.

Michelangelo called these figures Prigioni, prisoners. The word "slaves" was attached in the nineteenth century.

He gave the pair to Roberto Strozzi in 1544 and 1546, in thanks for hospitality during two illnesses. Strozzi, exiled for opposing Cosimo de' Medici, took them to Lyon in 1550 and presented them to Henri II of France, who passed them to the Constable Anne de Montmorency for his château at Écouen. Cardinal Richelieu bought them in 1632 for his château in Poitou. Bernini sketched them there. In 1749 they came to Paris, and after the Revolution they became state property.

## Look for

- The bonds. A thin strap across the chest, carved so lightly that you can miss it. It is the only thing identifying the figure as captive.
- The monkey behind the left knee. If the Slaves personify human beings trapped in physical existence, the monkey is material life. If they personify the Arts mourning the Pope, the monkey is Painting, which was traditionally called the ape of nature.
- The back. The Louvre's own guide tells you to walk all the way round, and it is right. The side and rear views are much rougher than the front.
- The unfinished block behind him, from which he has not fully emerged.

## Interesting facts

- Michelangelo owned a cast or copy of the Laocoön, dug up in Rome in 1506, and its influence on the twisting torso is direct.
- A letter from Michelangelo to Marcello dei Covi in 1513 mentions Luca Signorelli seeing the figures, which is how the date is fixed.
- The figures were designed to stand against pilasters on the lower storey of the tomb, which is why the fronts are finished and the sides are not.

## Talking point

Decide whether he is dying, sleeping or in ecstasy. Four centuries of scholars have not agreed.
