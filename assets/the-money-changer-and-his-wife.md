# The Money Changer and His Wife

Quentin Matsys, 1514. Richelieu wing, Level 2, in the early Netherlandish rooms.

## In one line

A man weighs gold, his wife turns a page of her prayer book and looks at the gold, and a convex mirror on the table catches a third person who is not in the room.

## What you are looking at

A couple behind a table. He is testing coins on a small balance, his eyes on the scale pan. She is turning the page of an illuminated Book of Hours, open at a page showing the Virgin and Child, and her eyes have drifted to the money.

On the table: coins, pearls, rings, a small weight box, a glass flask, a brass container. On the shelves behind: a snuffed candle, an apple, books, a carafe, a rosary, scales.

### The mirror

At the front edge of the table stands a small convex mirror in a wooden frame. In it, reversed and distorted, a man in a red turban sits at a window reading, with a church spire behind him through the glass.

He is not in the room the painting shows. He is in the space where you are standing. The mirror puts a viewer into the picture, and the device comes directly from van Eyck's Arnolfini Portrait of 1434.

## The story

The original frame carried an inscription from Leviticus 19:36: "Let the balance be just and the weights equal." The frame is lost.

The painting is usually read as a warning: the wife's attention slides from devotion to gold. But Matsys paints the couple with no contempt. They are respectable Antwerp burghers, and Antwerp in 1514 was the richest trading city in northern Europe, running on exactly this business. Whether the painting condemns them or simply describes them is still argued.

## Look for

- The mirror, and the reader inside it.
- The reflection on the brass and the glass, each surface with its own quality of light.
- The Book of Hours, painted well enough that the miniature is legible.
- Her hand, stopped mid-turn.
- The snuffed candle, a standard vanitas sign.

## Interesting facts

- Marinus van Reymerswaele reworked the composition about twenty-five years later, and his versions are in several museums. Matsys's is the original.
- The Antwerp collector Cornelis van der Geest owned it, and had it painted into a gallery picture by Willem van Haecht, so we know exactly where it hung.
- Van der Geest also paid for a memorial plaque to Matsys at Antwerp Cathedral.

## Talking point

The frame told you how to read the painting and the frame is gone. Decide without it.
