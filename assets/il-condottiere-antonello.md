# Portrait of a Man, known as Il Condottiere

Antonello da Messina, 1475. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 710 (Grande Galerie).

## In one line

A small panel with the hardest stare in the museum, and the man in it has never been identified.

## What you are looking at

A man of perhaps thirty-five, close up against a black ground, lit from the left. Dark cap, dark jerkin, a sliver of white shirt. A scar splits his upper lip. He is looking slightly past you and he does not like what he sees.

The panel is 36 by 30 centimetres. It holds a wall.

## The story

Antonello was the painter who carried the Netherlandish oil technique into Italy. He learned it in Naples from works by Jan van Eyck and Rogier van der Weyden, and his portraits combine Flemish precision in the surface with Italian structure in the head.

The scar and the expression earned it the nickname Il Condottiere, the mercenary captain, but no contemporary source calls him that and there is no evidence he was a soldier. The Louvre catalogues the sitter as possibly Giorgio Corner.

Napoleon III bought it in 1865, which is how it entered the collection and how it got its name.

## Look for

- The scar on the upper lip, slightly left of centre.
- The parapet at the bottom, a Netherlandish device, with a scrap of paper where Antonello signed and dated it.
- The stubble. Antonello paints individual bristles, which Italian tempera painters could not do.

## Interesting facts

- Antonello's arrival in Venice in 1475, the year of this painting, changed Venetian painting. Giovanni Bellini absorbed his method almost immediately, and Venetian oil painting follows from that.
- He died four years later, around 1479.
- The black background was rare in Italy at the time and standard in Flanders.

## Talking point

It is a postcard-sized panel. Stand a metre away and then two metres, and watch how much larger he gets.
