# The Winged Bulls of Khorsabad

Neo-Assyrian, c. 713–706 BC. Richelieu wing, Level 0, Room 229, the Cour Khorsabad.

## In one line

Count the legs.

## What you are looking at

Two colossal guardian figures, over four metres tall, with the body of a bull, the wings of an eagle, and the bearded head of a man wearing a horned crown. They flanked a gateway in the throne room complex of Sargon II's new capital.

A lamassu is a protective spirit. It stands at a threshold to keep out what should not enter. Between the legs of each figure runs a cuneiform inscription, part of Sargon's royal proclamation.

### The five legs

Each bull has five legs. From directly in front, you see two legs, planted, and the creature is standing at attention. From the side, you see four legs in motion, walking. The sculptor carved an extra leg so that both readings work, and there is no angle from which the figure looks wrong.

It is a relief carved on two faces of a corner block, solving a problem that a fully round sculpture could not.

## The story

Sargon II decided to build an entirely new capital from scratch, Dur-Sharrukin, "Fortress of Sargon", at what is now Khorsabad near Mosul. He died on campaign in 705 BC and the city was abandoned almost immediately, barely used.

Paul-Émile Botta, French consul in Mosul, excavated the site in 1843–1844. His finds went to Paris and opened the world's first Assyrian museum gallery in the Louvre in 1847. Some of what he shipped never arrived. Rafts carrying sculpture down the Tigris sank, and pieces are still at the bottom of the river.

## Look for

- The fifth leg, from the corner angle.
- The cuneiform between the legs.
- The horned crown, which marks divinity.
- The feather carving on the wings, repeated in tight regular ranks.
- The beard, arranged in tiers of curls.

## Interesting facts

- The Cour Khorsabad is a covered courtyard designed to give you the approach to the gateway. Stand back at the far end first.
- Comparable lamassu from Nimrud and Nineveh are in the British Museum, the Metropolitan Museum, and the Iraq Museum in Baghdad.
- ISIS destroyed lamassu at Nineveh and bulldozed Nimrud in 2015. The objects in Paris, London and New York are now a larger share of what survives than anyone would want them to be.

## Talking point

Walk from directly in front to directly to the side, slowly, and watch the animal start walking.
