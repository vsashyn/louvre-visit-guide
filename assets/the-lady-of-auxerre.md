# The Lady of Auxerre

Cretan, c. 650–625 BC. Denon wing, entresol level, Room 170 (Pre-Classical Greece), in a case.

## In one line

One of the oldest Greek statues in existence, and it was found in a storage cupboard in a provincial French museum with no record of how it got there.

## What you are looking at

A small limestone figure, 75 cm tall, standing rigid with her feet together. Her left arm hangs straight down, her right hand is pressed flat against her solar plexus. Her hair falls in heavy geometric locks. She is smiling the archaic smile, the fixed upward curve that Greek sculptors used for the next century and a half.

The proportions are deliberately flat below the waist and the waist itself is extremely narrow, a hangover from Minoan and Mycenaean taste.

## The story

In 1907 Maxime Collignon, a Louvre curator, found her in a storage vault at the museum in Auxerre in Burgundy. Nobody could say where she had come from or when she arrived. She had apparently been used as a prop in a theatre at some point.

The style is Daedalic, named after the mythical craftsman Daedalus, and belongs to the seventh century BC. Excavations in the 1990s by Nikolaos Stampolidis at Eleutherna in Crete produced closely comparable work, which strengthened the case for a Cretan origin in the Eleutherna and Gortyn region.

## Look for

- The traces of paint. She was brightly coloured, and patterns are still faintly visible on the skirt.
- The hand on the chest, a gesture of prayer or offering.
- The single block. Figure and base are carved from one piece of limestone.
- The hair, which shows Egyptian influence. Greek monumental sculpture begins with Greeks looking at Egyptian statues.

## Interesting facts

- She is roughly two centuries older than the Parthenon frieze a few rooms away, and about five centuries older than the Venus de Milo.
- Painted reconstructions exist, including one at the Museum of Classical Archaeology in Cambridge, and they are startling.
- Her provincial-museum origin story is genuinely unexplained. There is no acquisition record.

## Talking point

She is the oldest thing in this guide except the Egyptian and Mesopotamian objects. Put her next to the Venus de Milo in your head and the gap is 500 years.
