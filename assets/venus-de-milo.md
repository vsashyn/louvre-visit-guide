# Aphrodite, known as the Venus de Milo

Greek, c. 150–125 BC. Sully wing, Level 0, Room 346.

## In one line

Found in a field on a Greek island in 1820, and the inscription that named her sculptor went missing almost immediately.

## What you are looking at

A goddess, over two metres tall, nude to the hips with drapery falling from them and held, impossibly, in place. She turns in a slow spiral, hips one way, shoulders the other. The marble is carved in two main blocks joined at the drapery line.

Her expression is deliberately neutral and remote. Hellenistic sculptors could do violent emotion, as the Winged Victory shows. Here they chose calm.

## The story

On 8 April 1820 a farmer named Yorgos Kentrotas was clearing stone on Melos and uncovered her in a niche. The French naval officer Olivier Voutier was on the island, saw the dig and encouraged him to keep going. Jules Dumont d'Urville arrived shortly after, recognised what it was and set the wheels in motion for France to buy it.

She was presented to Louis XVIII, who gave her to the Louvre in 1821. The timing mattered. After Waterloo the Treaty of Vienna forced France to return around 5,000 looted works, and the Louvre had lost most of its great antiquities. The Venus arrived exactly when the museum needed a new icon, and she was promoted accordingly.

### The missing inscription

A plinth found with the statue carried an inscription naming the sculptor as "[Alex]andros of Antioch on the Maeander". It was recorded in a drawing by Auguste Debay, then disappeared. The most common explanation is that the date it implied, late Hellenistic, was unwelcome to curators who wanted her to be Classical Greek, from the age of Praxiteles. Whether or not that is why it vanished, it vanished.

## Look for

- The join line across the hips where the two marble blocks meet.
- The holes in the marble. Metal jewellery, a bracelet, earrings, a headband, was once fitted.
- The left foot, added later.
- The back, which is more roughly finished, suggesting she stood against a wall or in a niche.

## Interesting facts

- Best current reconstruction, supported by a 2010 analysis: the right hand held the slipping drapery, the left was raised holding an apple. A marble hand holding an apple was found with her. Melos, Milos, and mēlon, the Greek for apple, make a pun on the island's name.
- The position of the left leg has also been read as supporting a shield, which would make her the Aphrodite of Capua type.
- She was evacuated to the Château de Valençay during the Second World War and a plaster cast stood in her place.
- She has left France exactly once, for an exhibition in Tokyo and Kyoto in 1964.
- Kenneth Clark called her "the last great work of antique Greece".
- Over seventy published poems have been written about her.

## Practical

She now stands nearly alone in the middle of the room, visible down a long sightline from the corridor. Approach from a distance first, then circle her.

## Talking point

Reconstruct the arms yourselves. Stand behind her and copy the twist of the body, and you will feel where the weight has to go.
