# Portrait of Baldassare Castiglione

Raphael, c. 1514–1515. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 710 (Grande Galerie).

## In one line

The portrait that taught European painters how to paint a gentleman, and that Rembrandt sketched at an auction and then reused for the rest of his life.

## What you are looking at

Baldassare Castiglione, diplomat, humanist and author of The Book of the Courtier, sits facing you in winter clothes. A black doublet trimmed with grey squirrel fur, a soft black turban-like cap, a notched beret. The palette is almost entirely greys, blacks and browns against a neutral ground, with one accent: the blue-grey of his eyes.

Castiglione's book gave Europe the word sprezzatura, the art of making difficulty look easy. Raphael painted the idea.

## The story

The two men were friends. Castiglione probably advised Raphael on the programme of The School of Athens in the Vatican. He wrote a poem in the voice of his absent wife consoling herself with this portrait while he was posted to Rome.

Cardinal Mazarin owned it, and Louis XIV bought it from Mazarin's heirs in 1661.

## Look for

- The restraint. Almost no colour, almost no props, and it still reads as a full character study.
- The hands, folded low in the frame, the same pose Leonardo used for the Mona Lisa.
- The pyramid again. Raphael builds even a bust portrait on a triangle.

## Interesting facts

- Rembrandt saw this painting at an Amsterdam auction in 1639 and made a quick pen sketch of it, which survives. He then used the pose, the cap and the lighting in a run of his own self-portraits.
- Rubens made a painted copy, now at the Courtauld in London.
- It is one of only two Raphael portraits painted on canvas rather than panel.
- Titian saw it in Castiglione's house in Mantua and borrowed from it.

## Talking point

The colour range here is narrower than almost any painting nearby. Try to name more than five hues.
