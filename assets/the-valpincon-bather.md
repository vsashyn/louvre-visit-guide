# The Valpinçon Bather

Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, 1808. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 702 (Salle Daru).

## In one line

A back, a turban and a green curtain, painted by a 28-year-old, and Ingres spent the next fifty-five years repeating it.

## What you are looking at

A woman sits on the edge of a bed, seen from behind, a striped turban wound around her head. A heavy green curtain hangs on the left. In the lower right, a spout of water runs into a basin, the only movement in the picture.

There is no narrative, no mythology, no excuse for the nudity. Ingres simply paints a back.

## The story

Ingres made it in Rome while a student at the French Academy, as one of the works students were required to send back to Paris. It was not well received.

It was originally called Seated Woman, and took its present name from a nineteenth-century owner.

## Look for

- The skin. Ingres eliminates brushwork entirely. Robert Rosenblum described the effect as the figure floating weightlessly on enamel smoothness.
- The turban, wound in a precise spiral. Ingres loved this kind of drawn pattern.
- The water spout. Without it the picture would be completely silent.
- The sheet. Its folds are as carefully constructed as the body.

## Interesting facts

- Ingres reused this figure for the rest of his life. She reappears at the centre foreground of The Turkish Bath, painted in 1863 when he was 83.
- Man Ray's 1924 photograph Le Violon d'Ingres, which painted f-holes onto a woman's back, is a direct joke about this painting.
- Baudelaire noted the paradox that the figure is intensely sensual and completely chaste at the same time.

## Talking point

There is no face. Decide whether that makes the picture more or less intimate.
