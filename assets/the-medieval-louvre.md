# The Medieval Louvre

Built c. 1190–1202. Sully wing, Level -1, beneath the Cour Carrée.

## In one line

You can walk in the dry moat of a twelfth-century castle, under the museum, and touch the base of a tower that was demolished five hundred years ago.

## What you are looking at

The excavated foundations of Philippe Auguste's fortress. A curving stone wall, a paved moat floor you walk along, the circular base of the great keep, and the lower walls of two towers.

The stonework is huge, dry and pale, and the masons' marks are still on it. You are below the level of the Cour Carrée, walking around the outside of a castle that sits inside the palace.

## The story

Around 1190, before leaving on the Third Crusade, Philippe Auguste built a wall around Paris and a fortress at its western weak point, on the Seine, facing the direction from which the Normans and later the English would come. The Louvre was a defensive keep outside the city wall, not a residence.

At its centre stood the Grosse Tour, a cylindrical keep about 15 metres across and 30 metres high, surrounded by its own dry moat inside the outer walls. It was where the royal treasury and the archives were kept, and where important prisoners were held.

Charles V turned the fortress into a palace after 1364, cutting windows into the walls and adding staircases and gardens. It became known as the joli Louvre.

In the 1540s Francis I demolished the keep to open up the courtyard, and commissioned Pierre Lescot to rebuild the west range in Renaissance style. The Lescot wing, on the west side of the Cour Carrée, is the oldest standing part of the Louvre above ground.

### How it was found

Excavations for the Grand Louvre project in 1984 and 1985, digging out space for the new underground entrance, uncovered the moat, the keep base and the tower footings. Rather than clearing them, the architects built the new museum around them. The medieval rooms opened in 1989.

## Look for

- The base of the Grosse Tour, a complete circle of masonry.
- The masons' marks cut into individual blocks, the signatures of the men paid by the stone.
- The Salle Saint-Louis, a vaulted thirteenth-century room with surviving columns and capitals, the only intact medieval interior in the building.
- Objects found in the excavation, including a gilded parade helmet associated with Charles VI.
- The change in stone colour where the moat floor meets the wall.

## Interesting facts

- The name Louvre is unexplained. Proposals include Frankish for a fortified place, Latin for a leper house, and Old French for a wolf hunting kennel. None is proven.
- The fortress was outside the city wall when it was built. Paris grew past it within a century.
- The Pyramid entrance sits roughly where the western approach to the castle was.

## Practical

Quiet, cool, and usually empty. A good first or last stop, and an excellent place to escape the crowds around the Venus de Milo directly above.

## Talking point

The museum is the eighth or ninth building on this site. Ask how many of the eight hundred years of demolitions were considered vandalism at the time.
