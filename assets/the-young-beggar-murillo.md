# The Young Beggar

Bartolomé Esteban Murillo, c. 1645–1650. Denon wing, Level 1, Spanish painting rooms.

## In one line

A barefoot boy picking lice out of his shirt in a shaft of Seville sunlight, painted with the seriousness usually reserved for saints.

## What you are looking at

A boy sits on the ground in a bare room. Light comes from a window at the upper left and falls across his back and his dirty feet. Beside him, a clay pot, a basket, some shrimp shells and an apple. He is delousing himself, absorbed and unbothered.

Murillo gives him the full apparatus of serious painting: a strong diagonal light, a carefully balanced still life, a monumental silhouette. No sentimentality and no moral.

## The story

Seventeenth-century Seville was hit by plague, war and mass child abandonment. Murillo, who spent his life there and lost most of his own children young, painted street children repeatedly.

This is thought to be the first of them. The dealer Lebrun acquired it and it entered the royal collection of Louis XVI in 1782, making Murillo one of only three Spanish painters in the pre-Revolutionary French royal holdings.

## Look for

- The feet. The single dirtiest, most specific thing in the room.
- The shrimp shells on the floor. He has eaten.
- The apple in the basket, uneaten. He is keeping it.
- The light. It does all the composition.

## Interesting facts

- The alternative title is The Lice-Ridden Boy.
- It was criticised at first for being too idealised, and only properly admired from the Rococo onward.
- The subject belongs to the Spanish picaresque tradition, the same world as the novels of Cervantes and the anonymous Lazarillo de Tormes.

## Practical

The Spanish painting rooms in Denon, 720 to 734, have had extended partial closures. Check the Louvre's open-galleries list before you walk to this end of the wing, because it is a long way back.

## Talking point

Murillo spent most of his career painting Immaculate Conceptions for churches. This is what he did when nobody was commissioning.
