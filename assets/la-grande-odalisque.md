# La Grande Odalisque

Jean-Auguste-Dominique Ingres, 1814. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 702 (Salle Daru).

## In one line

A nude with roughly five extra vertebrae, and Ingres knew exactly what he was doing.

## What you are looking at

A woman reclines with her back to you in a curtained interior, turning her head over her shoulder to meet your eye. A peacock-feather fan in one hand, a turban, a pipe and an incense burner at her feet. Odalisque means a woman in a harem, and Ingres had never been anywhere near one.

The body is a long unbroken curve from the shoulder to the feet. To get that curve, Ingres lengthened the spine.

## The story

Commissioned by Caroline Murat, Queen of Naples and Napoleon's sister, in 1813. Ingres painted it in Rome. By the time it was shown at the Paris Salon in 1819 the Murats had fallen with the Empire, and the picture arrived without its context.

The critics went after the anatomy. One wrote that the figure had "neither bones nor muscle, neither blood, nor life, nor relief, indeed nothing that constitutes imitation."

## Look for

- The back. Contemporary critics said two or three vertebrae too many. A 2004 study in the medical literature put it at about five, with the pelvis rotated into a position that is anatomically impossible.
- The left arm, noticeably shorter than the right.
- The left leg, which does not credibly connect to the hip.
- The skin. Ingres polishes the paint until there is no brushwork at all, which is why the figure reads as porcelain rather than flesh.

## Interesting facts

- The pose comes almost directly from David's Portrait of Madame Récamier, hanging in the same suite of rooms. Ingres worked in David's studio.
- The Louvre bought it in 1899, 85 years after it was painted and 32 years after Ingres died.
- The Guerrilla Girls put a gorilla mask on her for their 1989 poster asking whether women have to be naked to get into the Met. Fewer than 5 per cent of the artists in the museum's modern sections were women; 85 per cent of the nudes were female.
- Martial Raysse turned her into a pop artwork in 1964.

## Talking point

Cover the head and the figure reads as pure design. Uncover it and she is looking straight at you, which is the whole trick.
