# La Belle Ferronnière

Leonardo da Vinci, c. 1490–1496. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 710 (Grande Galerie).

## In one line

A portrait whose title is a mistake, whose sitter is unidentified, and which once won a woman 60,000 dollars in a New York courtroom.

## What you are looking at

A young woman at the Milanese court of Ludovico Sforza, turned three-quarters, with her eyes cut sharply to the side. A stone parapet cuts across the bottom, blocking you out. The effect is of someone who has just looked up at a person standing to your right, and is about to look away again.

Compare her to the Mona Lisa. Here Leonardo is after a live instant, not a suspended mood.

## The story

The name comes from a ferronnière, the thin band with a jewel worn across the forehead. In the 17th century the painting was confused with a portrait of a supposed mistress of Francis I who was married to a man called Le Ferron, and the wrong name stuck. The Louvre's own trail notes that the painter Ingres cemented the error when he copied the work and labelled it with the other painting's title.

The sitter may be Lucrezia Crivelli, a lady-in-waiting who became Ludovico Sforza's mistress. A 2011 National Gallery exhibition argued instead for Beatrice d'Este, Ludovico's wife. Nobody knows.

## Look for

- The band across her forehead that gave the picture its wrong name.
- The parapet. Leonardo uses it to push you back and make her unavailable.
- The red of the sleeve and the laced ribbon work, painted with an attention that suggests a court commission.

## Interesting facts

- In the 1920s Andrée Hahn tried to sell a copy of this painting to the Kansas City Art Institute as the original. The dealer Joseph Duveen declared it a copy without having seen it, and she sued him for slander.
- The trial was a sensation. Connoisseurs travelled to the Louvre to compare the two paintings side by side. The jury, deliberately chosen for knowing nothing about Leonardo, awarded Hahn 60,000 dollars plus costs.
- The Hahn copy finally sold at Sotheby's in 2010 for 1.5 million dollars, catalogued as "by a follower of Leonardo".
- Bernard Berenson refused the attribution and gave the painting to Bernardino de' Conti.
- It appears in French royal inventories before the Revolution, so it has been in Paris a very long time.

## Talking point

Follow her eyeline out of the frame. Leonardo has painted someone else into the room, and it is not you.
