# The Victory Stele of Naram-Sin

Akkadian, c. 2250 BC. Richelieu wing, Level 0, in the Mesopotamian rooms near the Code of Hammurabi.

## In one line

The first known image of a living king shown as a god, carved 4,250 years ago.

## What you are looking at

A pink limestone slab. Naram-Sin, grandson of Sargon of Akkad, climbs a wooded mountain at the head of his army. He is larger than everyone else. He wears a horned helmet. Under his feet are the bodies of the defeated Lullubi, a people of the Zagros mountains. One enemy falls backward with a spear through his throat. Another begs.

Above the king, in the empty upper part of the stone, are sun discs, the sign of the god Shamash.

### Why it matters

Two things break with everything before it.

First, the composition abandons the register system. Mesopotamian reliefs before this stack figures in horizontal bands. Here a single diagonal runs up the mountain, and all the figures move along it, looking upward at the king.

Second, the horned helmet. In Mesopotamian art, horns mean divinity, and they belong to gods. Naram-Sin wears them. He is the earliest known Mesopotamian ruler to declare himself a god during his own lifetime, and the stele says so in stone.

## The story

Originally set up at Sippar in Babylonia. Around 1155 BC the Elamite king Shutruk-Nakhunte raided and carried it off to Susa, the same campaign that took the Code of Hammurabi. He added his own inscription recording the theft.

Jacques de Morgan's French mission excavated it at Susa in 1898.

## Look for

- The horned helmet.
- The soldiers, marching in disciplined ranks, against the disordered heap of the enemy. Akkadian order against barbarian chaos, stated as clearly as possible.
- The two surviving sun discs. There were originally three.
- The later Elamite inscription cut into the stone, recording Shutruk-Nakhunte's capture of it.
- The missing top. The stele was around three metres tall.

## Interesting facts

- Some scholars argue the campaign shown is in Cilicia rather than the Zagros, based on the vessels carried by the defeated.
- Akkad, Naram-Sin's capital, has never been located. It is one of the great missing cities of archaeology.
- Both this and the Hammurabi stele were looted by the same Elamite king, six hundred years apart in their making, and both were found in the same Iranian city by the same French mission.

## Talking point

Two of the Louvre's greatest Mesopotamian objects are here because an Elamite king stole them 3,000 years ago. Ask what that does to arguments about repatriation.
