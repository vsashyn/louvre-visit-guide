# Saint Sebastian

Andrea Mantegna, c. 1480. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 710 (Grande Galerie).

## In one line

A martyr tied to a Roman ruin, painted by the most archaeologically obsessed artist of the fifteenth century.

## What you are looking at

Sebastian bound to a fluted classical column amid the rubble of an ancient triumphal arch, pierced with arrows, his eyes turned upward. At the lower left, two archers walk away, indifferent.

Mantegna paints stone like a sculptor. Everything, including the saint's body, has the hardness of carved marble. He viewed the figure from slightly below to make it loom.

## The story

The theme is God's Athlete, drawn from a sermon then attributed to Saint Augustine. Sebastian survived the arrows, which is why he was invoked against plague.

The painting was recorded in the Sainte-Chapelle of Aigueperse in the Auvergne, which it reached through the 1486 marriage of Chiara Gonzaga, daughter of Federico I of Mantua, to Gilbert de Bourbon, Dauphin d'Auvergne. Mantegna was court painter to the Gonzaga.

## Look for

- The two archers bottom left, walking off. Mantegna contrasts the man of faith with men interested only in getting paid.
- The ruins. Fragments of a Roman arch, carved capitals, a foot from a shattered statue.
- The fig tree growing out of the column, a detail of pure observation.
- The arrows. Count them, and note how carefully each entry wound is drawn.

## Interesting facts

- There are three Mantegna Saint Sebastians: Vienna, Venice (Ca' d'Oro) and this one. The Vienna version has the famous cloud horseman and a Greek signature. This one does not.
- Mantegna owned a collection of antiquities and studied Roman remains directly, which is why his ruins are specific rather than generic.
- He was Giovanni Bellini's brother-in-law, and the two influenced each other heavily.

## Talking point

Look at the stone and the flesh side by side. Mantegna gives them exactly the same surface, which is either a failure of observation or the entire point.
