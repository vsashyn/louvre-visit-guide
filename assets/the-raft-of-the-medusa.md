# The Raft of the Medusa

Théodore Géricault, 1818–1819. Denon wing, Level 1, Room 700 (Salle Mollien).

## In one line

A government scandal painted at the scale of a history painting, by a 27-year-old who shaved his head and locked himself in his studio for eight months.

## What you are looking at

Fifteen survivors on a makeshift raft in an ocean swell. The dead and dying make the base of a pyramid at the lower left. The living strain up and to the right, towards a man standing on a barrel waving a cloth at a speck on the horizon.

The rescue ship is barely visible. Géricault chose the moment before anyone knows whether they have been seen.

## The story

On 2 July 1816 the French frigate Méduse ran aground off Mauritania. Her captain, an émigré aristocrat appointed for political loyalty rather than seamanship, had not commanded a ship in over twenty years. The lifeboats took the officers. About 147 people were put on a raft that was supposed to be towed and was cut loose.

Thirteen days later fifteen were alive. They had endured thirst, starvation, murder, and cannibalism. Two survivors, the surgeon Henri Savigny and the engineer Alexandre Corréard, published an account that became a political scandal for the restored Bourbon monarchy.

Géricault interviewed both men. He built a scale model of the raft. He borrowed severed limbs and a head from hospitals and an asylum and kept them in his studio to study decay. He shaved his head so he would not go out. He finished in eight months.

## Look for

- The figure on the barrel is a Black man, based on a model named Joseph. Géricault was an abolitionist, and putting a Black man at the apex of a national history painting in 1819 was a deliberate statement.
- The old man at the lower left, holding a dead youth across his knees and staring out of the picture. He is the only one not looking at the horizon.
- The darkening. Géricault used bitumen, which contracts and turns to black treacle over time. Whole areas of detail are gone and cannot be recovered.
- Eugène Delacroix modelled for the face-down figure in the foreground. He was 20.

## Interesting facts

- At the 1819 Salon it split the critics. Louis XVIII reportedly told Géricault his shipwreck "is certainly no disaster." Neoclassicists called it a pile of corpses.
- Géricault took it to London in 1820, where 40,000 people paid to see it and he made about 20,000 francs, far more than the French state had offered.
- The Louvre bought it in 1824, just after his death at 32 following a riding accident.
- It was evacuated to the Château de Chambord in autumn 1939 ahead of the war.
- Because of the bitumen damage, the Louvre commissioned a full-size copy in 1859–60 from Pierre-Désiré Guillemet and Étienne Ronjat for exhibition loans.

## Talking point

Count the living. Then read the number who went onto the raft.
